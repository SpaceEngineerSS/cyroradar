# Depth and layers

The radar measures time. Depth needs a speed, and the speed depends on what the wave is travelling through. The depth model is where you say what that is.

## From delay to depth

In a medium with relative permittivity $\varepsilon$ the wave travels at $v = c/\sqrt{\varepsilon}$. Down and back through a layer of thickness $d$ takes

$$
\Delta t = \frac{2\,d\,\sqrt{\varepsilon}}{c}.
$$

With several layers the delays add up from the surface down. CryoRadar walks the stack until the delay is used up; below the last layer the last speed carries on, and with no layers at all a single bulk permittivity is used.

A permittivity of 3.15 for water ice is a reasonable start. Dusty ice is 3.3–3.5, dry regolith 3–5, basalt 7–9.

## Building a model

Open **Depth model**, choose a material and press **Add**. Each layer has a permittivity, a loss tangent and a thickness; picking a material fills in published values, and you can change them.

To tie the model to the radargram:

1. Make a layer active and press **Pick base** (or press `P`).
2. Click along the reflector at the bottom of that layer. Each click is a pick.
3. Press **Fit to picks**. The thickness is set so the base of the layer lands on the average delay of your picks, given the layers above it.

For simulated scenes, **Copy the scene's own model** loads the layers the simulator used.

## RMS velocity

Migration needs the velocity that best describes a hyperbola through several layers, which is the root-mean-square velocity of Dix (1955):

$$
V_\text{rms}(t)^2 = \frac{\sum_i v_i^2\,\Delta t_i}{\sum_i \Delta t_i}.
$$

The Depth model panel shows it for the whole stack, along with the two-way dielectric loss.

## Ice temperature

The permittivity of ice barely changes with temperature at radar frequencies, but its loss does. The **Ice temperature** section sets every water-ice layer from a temperature using a Debye relaxation plus an impurity conduction term (the equations are on the [physics page](/docs/physics#ice-at-mars-temperatures)). At 170 K clean ice loses very little; near 250 K the loss is several times higher.
